I came to statistics through biology. While I was finishing a mathematics degree at Stanford, the first draft of the human genome was nearing completion, microarrays were beginning to change how biological research was done, and the term “GWAS” had not yet been coined. The idea that quantitative models, applied to data from high-throughput experiments, could give a systemic understanding of biology was hard to resist.

Being a mathematics major, though, had rather little to do with knowing what to do with data. So I went on to a PhD in statistics, and picked up the biology I needed along the way.

How I think about research

Nearly everything I have worked on comes back to two questions:

  • What can the data from a particular experiment meaningfully tell us?
  • How can we answer that transparently, rigorously, and at scale?

The first is as much a question about the experiment as about the analysis, and it is the one that decides whether the second is worth asking.

Current interests

For the last several years the lab has worked mostly with single-cell and spatial transcriptomic data. Much of what we do concerns cell state — how it is defined, how it varies within a tissue or a tumor, and how it changes along a lineage or with age — together with the statistical questions that come with trying to measure any of that reliably.

The methodological threads run further back than that — copy number, deconvolution, change-point and nonparametric methods among them. They are easier to see in the publications, which can be filtered by topic.

Teaching and advising

I advise students in both Statistics and Data Science and the Graduate Group in Genomics and Computational Biology, and the lab has always been a mix of the two.
